On Marginalia, and Reading With a Pencil

There is a particular kind of honesty that only shows up in the margins of a book — not the tidy, composed thoughts you'd write in a review, but the immediate ones: a question mark next to a line you don't trust yet, an underline you can't fully explain, a scribbled "wait" three chapters before the twist actually lands. Reading with a pencil turns a book into a conversation instead of a lecture, and the evidence of that conversation is worth keeping.

I didn't always read this way. For years I treated books like borrowed things, even the ones I owned outright — spines uncracked, pages pristine, as if the highest compliment I could pay a story was to leave no trace of having been inside it. It took a secondhand copy of a novel, already annotated by a stranger, to change my mind. Their notes argued with the author. They circled a sentence on page forty and simply wrote "no." I have never felt closer to an unnamed reader in my life.

What the margins are actually for

A margin note doesn't need to be clever. Most of mine aren't — they're closer to a pulse than a thought: a bracket around a paragraph I'll want to find again, a single word that captures a feeling before it dissolves, a date, sometimes just a line. The point isn't to produce something quotable. It's to leave a record of attention, so that when I return to the book — and I always do — I'm not reconstructing my first reading from memory. I'm right back inside it.

This is, I think, the real case for writing in books: not that it helps you remember what you read, but that it lets a book keep changing after you've closed it. Every re-read becomes a conversation with an earlier version of yourself, held in the same margin, in different ink.
